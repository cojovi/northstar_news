October's inflation report has an unusually conspicuous blank. The Bureau of Labor Statistics can tell Americans that consumer prices rose during the month, but cannot publish most of the usual year-over-year comparisons because it did not collect the corresponding survey data during the October 2025 funding lapse.

The blank is a measurement problem, not a disappearance of inflation. In the November 10 release, BLS reports that the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers rose 0.4 percent in October on a seasonally adjusted basis, following September's 0.6 percent increase. The index excluding food and energy rose 0.3 percent.

Those figures describe current monthly price movement. They do not supply an official annual headline rate, and an analyst should not quietly manufacture one to make a familiar chart look complete.

For households, the useful question is what the report does establish. Shelter, food and energy all rose. A missing annual comparison does not make a grocery receipt or a rent payment less real. It does make careful explanation more important than another round of inflation slogans.

## **Missing the Comparison Is Not Missing the Month**

An annual percentage change needs observations at both ends of the comparison. BLS says that, for most CPI series, it could not publish October 2025 indexes because survey data were not collected during the lapse in appropriations. Where that starting point is unavailable, the corresponding October 2026 annual percentage change cannot be published.

That is different from saying BLS did not measure October 2026. The release contains current observations, monthly changes and detailed categories. The limitation concerns what those observations can be compared with over the full year.

Some categories in the release still have annual figures. The warning says “most,” not “all.” Readers should therefore avoid either extreme: inventing a complete annual rate or declaring every annual statistic in the report unavailable.

The proper treatment is to keep the published series and their qualifications together. A private estimate that fills a missing observation is an estimate, not a newly recovered government measurement. Its assumptions should be visible, and it should not be attributed to BLS as an official observation.

## **Shelter Is Still Doing Heavy Lifting**

BLS reports a 0.3 percent increase in the shelter index in October, accounting for more than thirty percent of the monthly all-items increase.

That contribution matters because it keeps the household story from collapsing into a gasoline story. Fuel prices are visible and politically potent, but the broader index also reflects expenses that do not appear on a roadside sign.

A national shelter index is not a prediction of the next rent notice sent to a particular tenant. Nor does its monthly movement establish what happened to every mortgage payment. The index describes the measured category, not a personalized bill for every household.

The practical conclusion is narrower and more useful: shelter was an important contributor to October's overall increase. Any explanation that ignores it leaves out a substantial part of the report's own account.

Affordability arguments need that kind of specificity. “Prices are high” describes an experience; identifying which categories are moving helps explain the current change. Neither task becomes easier by pretending the unavailable annual comparison is available.

## **Food Adds Pressure in More Than One Place**

The food index increased 0.5 percent during October. Food at home rose 0.4 percent, while food away from home rose 0.5 percent, according to the release.

That distinction separates grocery purchases from meals bought elsewhere. It also reminds readers that even a familiar phrase such as “food inflation” combines different spending patterns. A household cooking nearly every meal and a household frequently eating out do not necessarily experience the same mix.

The figures do not establish that every item on every shelf rose by those percentages. Categories contain variation, and national averages are not price promises at a particular store. A specific product can become cheaper while the broader category rises.

Still, the direction of the measured aggregate is clear. Food prices increased over the month. The absence of the usual annual comparison should not become an excuse to omit that result or to describe the monthly report as empty.

For readers managing a budget, separating the categories is more informative than trying to force a single national number to explain every receipt.

## **Energy Rose More Slowly, Not in Reverse**

The energy index rose 0.7 percent in October after September's 5.2 percent increase. That is a slower monthly increase. It is not a fall in the index.

This is the distinction that inflation commentary repeatedly loses. A lower rate of increase still means a higher measured price level over that comparison period. Relief from the pace of deterioration and a reversal of the deterioration are different things.

The release's table shows variation within energy as well: electricity and utility gas need not move together, and gasoline is not the whole category. A broad claim that all energy costs behaved identically would outrun the data.

Nor does one monthly index establish which policy caused the change. Taxes, supply, demand and other influences require their own evidence. The CPI measures price movement; it is not a ready-made causal verdict on every political dispute.

A serious assessment should recognize the slower energy increase while still explaining that it added to, rather than subtracted from, the monthly all-items movement.

## **Core Inflation Is Useful, but Nobody Lives on Core Alone**

The index excluding food and energy increased 0.3 percent, the same monthly increase reported for September and August. BLS identifies increases in categories including recreation, medical care, motor vehicle insurance, household furnishings and education. Used vehicles and communication were among the categories that declined.

Excluding food and energy can help analysts examine a different part of the price picture. It does not imply that families can exclude those expenses from their bank accounts. “Core” is an analytical grouping, not a recommended household shopping list.

The category movements also caution against declaring the entire economy uniformly more expensive at the same speed. Some prices decreased. The overall index rose because it combines categories using its methodology rather than selecting whichever individual change best suits a preferred argument.

For the same reason, a household whose largest expenses differ from the index's composition may experience a different personal inflation rate. That does not automatically invalidate either the household's experience or the national statistic. They are different measurements answering related questions.

## **The Fed Question Is Not a Decision Announcement**

The report is evidence relevant to monetary policy, not a Federal Reserve decision. A reader can reasonably see continuing monthly increases as a concern without asserting that a particular rate move has already happened or is mathematically inevitable.

The missing annual comparison makes disciplined use of the available monthly evidence particularly important. It does not authorize replacing uncertainty with a confident invented figure. Nor should a market expectation be confused with an enacted policy change.

The institutional lesson reaches beyond a single inflation argument. Reliable public statistics are basic government infrastructure. A funding lapse can leave a measurement gap that persists into later comparisons, even after a new month's data collection is functioning.

Taxpayers deserve competent measurement and candid explanation of its limits. In this release, the most defensible account is straightforward: prices rose in October, shelter and food remained important, energy increased more slowly than in September, and most official annual comparisons were unavailable. The missing number should be acknowledged. The numbers that are present should still be read.
