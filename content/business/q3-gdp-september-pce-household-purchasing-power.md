The American economy can grow briskly while a household finds that its paycheck buys less. That is not a contradiction, and it is not necessarily evidence that either the growth figures or the household's frustration is imaginary.

Two reports released by the Bureau of Economic Analysis on October 29 put that distinction in unusually clear focus. Inflation-adjusted gross domestic product expanded at a 3.4 percent annualized rate in the third quarter. Separately, inflation-adjusted disposable personal income fell 0.3 percent in September, while real consumer spending increased less than 0.1 percent.

The national production engine was moving. At the end of the quarter, purchasing power was going backward. Before anyone turns either number into a campaign slogan, it is worth opening the hood.

## The Growth Number Is Real—and It Is Not a Pay Raise

The advance estimate puts third-quarter real GDP growth above the second quarter's 2.2 percent annualized pace. BEA identified higher investment, consumer spending and government spending as contributors, partly offset by lower exports. Imports increased.

There is an important unit attached to that headline. Annualized growth describes the rate that would result if the quarter's pace continued for a year. It does not mean the economy produced 3.4 percent more in three months. BEA also reports the quarter-to-quarter increase as 0.8 percent.

Nor does GDP tell us how much the typical worker's paycheck rose, how income was distributed, or whether a particular family could more comfortably meet its bills. It measures production across the economy. It is useful precisely because it has a defined job, not because it answers every economic question.

Dismissing a strong GDP report because households are unhappy is bad analysis. Treating the same report as proof that household complaints are unfounded is the mirror-image mistake.

## September's Spending Increase Bought Very Little More

The personal income report provides a closer look at the money moving through household budgets. Current-dollar personal consumption expenditures rose 0.5 percent in September. The PCE price index also rose 0.5 percent.

After adjusting for prices, spending increased less than 0.1 percent. BEA's summary table rounds that change to 0.0 percent. Those are compatible descriptions of a small increase, not conflicting reports.

Disposable income—income after personal current taxes—rose 0.2 percent in dollar terms. After inflation, it fell 0.3 percent. The difference between a bigger dollar figure and greater purchasing power is not academic when the same groceries, services and transport cost more.

These are aggregate estimates. They do not establish that every household lost purchasing power or that every category became more expensive. They do establish why rising nominal income and rising nominal spending should not automatically be presented as evidence that living standards improved during that month.

## A Strong Quarter Can End With a Softer Month

The GDP estimate covers July through September. The income report isolates September. A quarter can show substantial growth even when its final month is less encouraging.

Confusing these time periods creates a particularly easy political trick: pick the stronger quarterly result or the weaker monthly result and pretend the other does not exist. Neither choice gives readers the complete picture.

The price measures also require care. BEA says the PCE price index rose at a 2.1 percent annualized rate across the third quarter. Its separate monthly report puts September PCE inflation at 3.6 percent compared with a year earlier, with prices rising 0.5 percent from August.

Those figures use different comparison periods. They cannot be substituted for each other, and the quarterly figure does not show that annual inflation has reached the Federal Reserve's 2 percent goal. The appropriate question is what the measures reveal together: quarterly moderation in prices did not prevent renewed pressure at the quarter's end.

## Investment Is Doing Important Work

BEA's technical notes identify private inventories and equipment as important components of investment growth. Information-processing equipment, mainly computers and peripheral equipment, led the increase in equipment.

Reuters reported that business equipment spending rose at a 20.9 percent annualized rate, with information-processing equipment accounting for much of the contribution. Its reporting links the investment expansion to the AI buildout.

That connection matters, but it should not swallow the entire story. A computer purchase classified in the national accounts is not, by itself, proof of a specific company's AI deployment or future profits. The primary release establishes the investment categories; the broader AI interpretation comes from reporting about business activity.

Productive investment can improve future capacity and efficiency. It can also disappoint investors. Today's expenditure is not a guarantee of tomorrow's return. The sensible response is neither to sneer at growth nor to assume every new machine will pay for itself.

## Imports Are an Accounting Adjustment, Not a Moral Verdict

BEA subtracts imports in calculating GDP. That wording routinely gets translated into the claim that foreign purchases simply destroy American growth. The accounting is more specific.

Consumer spending and investment can include imported products. Subtracting imports prevents foreign production from remaining counted as domestic output. A larger import bill may therefore produce a negative contribution in the GDP calculation while accompanying strong domestic purchasing activity.

This does not settle arguments about industrial dependence, supply-chain security or trade policy. Those are legitimate questions with evidence of their own. It does mean that the national accounts should not be asked to deliver a political verdict their arithmetic does not contain.

For a country weighing the resilience of its industrial base, separating an accounting identity from a strategic judgment is a useful start.

## The Fed Has Not Declared Victory

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.75 to 4 percent on October 28. The policy statement described economic activity as expanding at a strong pace and inflation as remaining elevated.

The vote was 10–2. The two dissenters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, preferred a quarter-point increase—not a cut. That detail matters when interpreting a decision to hold as evidence that policymakers are comfortable with inflation.

The statement does not promise the next move. Neither the GDP report nor September's income figures establishes what the Fed will decide later. Forecasts of a rate increase are forecasts, even when economists can explain the case for them.

For borrowers, the practical point is less dramatic: a resilient economy does not guarantee imminent relief from interest costs. Price stability remains unfinished work.

## Judge Prosperity With More Than One Gauge

The saving rate was 3.8 percent in September. Together with falling real disposable income and almost flat real spending, that offers a more restrained picture of household flexibility than the GDP headline alone.

It is not enough to establish a recession, widespread insolvency or an inevitable spending collapse. One month's national estimate cannot diagnose every family's finances. The GDP number is also an advance estimate and is subject to revision.

But fiscal and monetary claims should face a basic accountability test: are policymakers discussing production, purchasing power, prices, or borrowing costs—and are they using the correct time period? The measures are related. They are not interchangeable.

The economy's strength is worth acknowledging. So is the household budget's discomfort. Competent economic policy should be able to hold both facts in view without treating either as an inconvenience.
