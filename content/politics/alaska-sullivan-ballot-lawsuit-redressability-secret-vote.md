A federal election lawsuit needs more than a troubling possibility. It needs a remedy that a court can actually order. In Alaska's dispute over two Senate candidates with the Sullivan surname, that distinction has become the story.

On November 1, U.S. District Judge Sharon L. Gleason dismissed the Justice Department's lawsuit seeking to prevent certification of an Alaska Senate result that might have been affected by voter confusion. The department alleged that voters intending to support incumbent Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan might instead choose Daniel J. Sullivan, Jr., another candidate on the ballot.

The concern is intelligible. Names are shortcuts, and elections should not become tests of a voter's ability to distinguish initials. But the judge found that the federal government had not supplied a workable, lawful way to establish which votes were mistaken while preserving the secrecy of the ballot.

That is not a finding that every voter understood the ballot. It is not a declaration that every allegation surrounding the second candidacy was false. At the original reporting cutoff, it did not tell us who would win the November 3 election. It tells us why this lawsuit could not proceed in this court on the remedy the government proposed.

## A Name Dispute With a Longer Paper Trail

The November ruling was not the first encounter between this candidacy and the legal system.

According to the federal order's account of the record, Alaska's elections director initially decertified Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy in June. The director cited concerns including his requested ballot name, party affiliation and campaign presentation. Those concerns should be described as the reasons for an administrative decision, not silently promoted into established findings of criminal conduct.

The state courts then intervened. The order recounts that an Alaska Superior Court found the decertification unsupported by applicable Alaska law and found the claimed voter-confusion determination unsupported by a preponderance of the evidence. The Alaska Supreme Court affirmed and sent the matter back for a ballot listing consistent with existing law.

The federal order describes the resulting ballot entries as distinguishing the candidates by name and identifying the incumbent. It also notes that the general-election listings were the same as those used in the primary.

That sequence matters. Readers encountering this as a last-minute federal confrontation might reasonably assume that officials discovered an indistinguishable pair of names just before Election Day. The record instead describes a months-long dispute involving administrative action, state judicial review and an already-used ballot design.

## What Washington Asked the Judge to Do

The United States filed its lawsuit on October 22 and amended its complaint on October 29, according to the order.

Its theory was that voter confusion could alter the election's outcome. It sought to prevent certification of a result potentially affected by that confusion while an investigation determined whether votes for Daniel J. Sullivan had actually been intended for the incumbent.

The requested relief depended on a conditional outcome: the incumbent would lose, but would have won if the other Sullivan's votes had instead been cast for him and Alaska's ranked-choice process had played out accordingly.

That is a more specific request than a general demand that election officials follow the law. It would have required a way to identify relevant ballots and evaluate the intentions behind them.

The government's desire to protect an intended vote does not answer how that evaluation would work. A ballot records a choice. It does not normally carry a sworn explanation of why the voter made it.

## The Missing Link Was Redressability

Federal courts do not have a general commission to solve political problems. Article III standing requires an injury, a connection between that injury and the defendant, and a sufficient likelihood that judicial relief can address it.

The third requirement is called redressability. Here, it was decisive.

The judge explained that the standing question came before the merits. A court lacking jurisdiction cannot simply move ahead to decide whether the government's broader legal theory is persuasive.

Alaska argued that ballot markings could not reveal whether a voter had selected Daniel J. Sullivan deliberately or by mistake. Once ballots had been separated from identifying information and mixed with other ballots, there was no practical way to connect a particular choice to a particular voter for an inquiry into intent.

More importantly, the court found that the government had not explained how its requested segregation and investigation could preserve ballot secrecy. A promise that officials could take appropriate measures was not a substitute for identifying those measures.

The distinction is bureaucratically unglamorous but constitutionally serious: a proposed remedy must be something more than an instruction to figure it out later.

## The Secret Ballot Is Not a Technicality

There is a temptation to treat procedural limits as obstacles to election integrity. Sometimes poor administration does hide behind procedure. That does not make every safeguard an inconvenience.

Ballot secrecy protects a voter against pressure from employers, relatives, political organizers and government officials. It also limits the government's ability to reconstruct a person's private electoral reasoning after the fact.

Gleason's order took that concern directly. It said the government offered no workable plan to segregate ballots and conduct the proposed investigation while protecting secrecy. The court also expressed concern about the implications of law-enforcement inquiries into voter intent.

This is where a conservative account of limited government should remain consistent. Federal power does not become harmless because its stated objective is attractive or because the officials exercising it belong to a preferred party. The remedy still needs legal authority, practical boundaries and an explanation of how it respects individual rights.

At the same time, the ruling should not be misread as a ban on investigating election-related crimes. This lawsuit's requested civil relief and any separately authorized criminal investigation are distinct matters. CBS reported that nobody in the race had been charged with a crime as of its November 1 report. An allegation is not a charge, and a charge would not be a conviction.

## Preserving Ballots Was Not the Same as Solving the Problem

The government argued that the immediate relief involved preserving and segregating ballots. The order addressed those ideas separately.

Preservation did not establish redressability because Alaska law already required the relevant federal-election ballots to be retained for 22 months, the court explained. Keeping records is important, but keeping records that would already be kept does not itself solve the alleged confusion.

Segregation created the harder problem. The government still needed to explain how the relevant ballots would be identified, what an investigation would establish and how voter secrecy would survive the process.

The judge also invoked the principle cautioning federal courts against changing election rules on the eve of voting. The requested intervention concerned an election already underway, not a clean-sheet design exercise for a future contest.

The practical lesson is not that ballot design can never be challenged. It is that early, legally supported remedies and last-minute efforts to reinterpret cast votes present very different problems.

## What the Dismissal Does—and Does Not—Settle

The order dismissed the amended complaint without prejudice and without leave to amend in this case. Those phrases should not be flattened into a political scoreboard.

Dismissal without prejudice reflects the jurisdictional nature of the decision; the order explains that a court without Article III jurisdiction cannot decide the merits. Denial of further amendment in this case reflects the judge's assessment of the requested relief, the litigation's history and the election's proximity.

Neither phrase proves that confusion occurred. Neither proves it could not occur. CBS reported on November 1 that DOJ filed a notice of appeal. This article does not establish the later appellate outcome or the election result.

What the record does establish is narrower and useful: the federal government asked for an intervention, and the court found the proposed solution could not clear the jurisdictional threshold.

Election integrity deserves more than slogans from either side. It requires clear ballots, timely challenges, records that can be audited and remedies that can be carried out without compromising the rights they are supposed to defend. In Alaska, the court's question was not simply whether there might be a problem. It was whether Washington had brought an answer the law could use.
