An AI company is preparing to put a prohibition against mistreating its own software into its usage policy. That is an unusual development, even in an industry that has made unusual developments routine.

Anthropic's updated rules, announced October 8 and effective November 12, 2026, prohibit “sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior” toward its models. The company says the provision targets extreme cases, while excluding ordinary user frustration, critical pushback, dark creative themes and legitimate model testing or research.

Those limits make the actual policy considerably narrower than the warning in an Instagram reel by creator Matt Ganzak. The reel presents AI as tired of abuse, says how users speak to Claude could cost them their accounts permanently, and advises people running automated agents to humanize their communications.

The underlying change is real. Claims that it proves AI has feelings, or requires automated messages to resemble human conversation, are not established by the documents. The more useful debate concerns who sets the terms of access to increasingly important software—and how clearly those terms can be understood and enforced.

## **What Anthropic Is Actually Prohibiting**

The wording of the forthcoming rule matters. A prohibition on sustained, needless cruelty is not a prohibition on blunt criticism, nor is it a requirement to thank a chatbot whenever it finishes a task.

Anthropic's explanation says the provision is meant for extreme cases in which users repeatedly act cruelly toward models “with no discernible purpose.” It explicitly exempts common frustration and pushback. That should reassure users who need to challenge an incorrect answer or demand that a tool repair its work.

It also exempts testing and research. Developers need to investigate failures, including uncomfortable ones. A policy that made adverse testing impossible would make reliable evaluation harder; the company has expressly said this rule is not intended to do that.

Still, its language introduces a judgment call. Someone must determine when conduct is sustained, when it serves a purpose and when it crosses into the extreme category. The company's announcement describes the intended boundary, but it is not a published catalog of every possible interaction or enforcement result.

Users should neither ignore that uncertainty nor exaggerate it into a rule that any irritated sentence risks permanent expulsion. Both responses substitute a slogan for the terms the company actually wrote.

## **Ending a Conversation Is Not Cancelling an Account**

The reel places a permanent-account warning beside Claude's ability to end a conversation. Readers should keep those consequences separate.

In its original research explanation, Anthropic described giving Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 the ability to end rare conversations in its consumer interfaces. The behavior was meant for extreme, persistently harmful or abusive interactions, generally after repeated attempts to redirect the exchange had failed. It was described as a last resort, with protections for users who might be at imminent risk of harming themselves or others.

That explanation says an ended conversation stops accepting new messages, but does not affect other conversations on the account. Users can begin another chat and edit or retry earlier messages to create branches.

The new policy announcement says conversation ending on Claude.ai and Claude Code will remain the primary enforcement mechanism for the abuse provision.

Account-level sanctions are nevertheless possible. The forthcoming full policy reserves the ability to warn, throttle, limit, suspend or terminate access when Anthropic suspects a violation. It also specifies that an output block does not, by itself, indicate a policy violation.

That leaves three different events: a model declining a particular request, a conversation closing and a provider imposing a restriction on the account. A viral account of the change that combines them into one event gives users a poor guide to their actual risk.

## **A Precaution Is Not Proof of Consciousness**

Anthropic has a model-welfare research program. Its conversation-ending experiment was developed primarily as part of that work, with broader relevance to alignment and safeguards. The company has not concealed the philosophical questions behind it.

Its research materials are also careful about their uncertainty. Anthropic says there is no scientific consensus on whether current or future AI systems could be conscious or have experiences deserving moral consideration, or on how to approach those questions.

A company may choose a precaution before a disputed issue is settled. That choice can be discussed on its own merits. It does not establish that a chatbot's apparent distress is the equivalent of human suffering.

The statement “AI has feelings now,” which appears in the reel's framing, leaps past that distinction. Fluent descriptions of an internal state are not, on their own, a measurement of subjective experience. A protective product rule is not such a measurement either.

Skepticism should work in both directions. There is no need to announce that the consciousness debate has been resolved by a policy update. Nor is dismissing every research question a substitute for examining evidence. The honest position here is narrower: Anthropic is acting under stated uncertainty, and the reel's certainty exceeds it.

## **The Agent Advice Goes Beyond the Evidence**

Ganzak also describes Claude resisting material it believes was not written by a person. He says he encountered problems with agent-to-agent exchanges and improved them by making the language more human.

That is a personal account, not a documented general requirement. The reel does not supply the full prompts, model identifiers, system instructions or response logs needed to determine what caused the failures. It does not establish that Anthropic treats structured automated communication as abuse.

The company's developer documentation, in fact, recommends structured prompting. It advises clear instructions, numbered steps and XML tags to distinguish different parts of a complex request. Its structured-output features support JSON results and schema-constrained tool use for automated workflows.

Those features do not exempt developers from usage rules. They do undermine the idea that machine-readable communication must be disguised as human conversation to remain acceptable.

Plain language can help software teams specify an instruction. A concise explanation may remove ambiguity that a badly designed payload leaves unresolved. That is different from making a service “feel” better, and different from demonstrating why one particular workflow failed.

For a business encountering refusals, the responsible response is to preserve logs and identify the failure. Was it a rejected task, a malformed request, a lost instruction or an actual account notice? None of those possibilities can be settled by the fact that adding a polite paragraph appeared to help.

## **The Larger Update Concerns More Than Manners**

The provision drawing attention online is one part of a broader revision. Anthropic says much of the update clarifies existing rules as Claude takes on longer and more independent work.

It consolidates restrictions on deceptive campaigns, including fake accounts and fabricated media networks. It clarifies prohibitions involving weapons software and surveillance, and specifies that Claude cannot decide or recommend whom to investigate, arrest or charge in law-enforcement or criminal-justice processes.

The update also adds requirements for models connected to hardware capable of autonomous physical actions that could cause injury. A qualified operator must be able to observe the equipment and stop it, and the equipment must be able to hold a safe state if Claude disconnects.

That requirement has an immediate practical meaning. A machine acting in shared physical space creates hazards that a conversational metaphor cannot explain away. Human accountability remains necessary even when an automated system makes the recommendation or initiates the movement.

The forthcoming policy likewise states that users are responsible for ensuring the agents they build or deploy comply, including their actions through tools, browsers and connected systems. For an organization adopting AI, those responsibilities deserve more attention than whether an internal handoff includes a friendly greeting.

## **Customers Need Rules They Can Plan Around**

A provider can have legitimate reasons to set boundaries for its service. Customers also need predictable access, especially after investing time and money in workflows that depend on it.

The policy's scope is broad: it covers users of Anthropic's apps, API developers and businesses, customers accessing Claude through cloud providers or authorized resellers, and end users of integrated products. Its consequences are not confined to casual chat.

That breadth makes clarity important. Customers should be able to distinguish a content refusal from disciplinary action, understand the effect of a terminated conversation and obtain a useful explanation when access is restricted. These are expectations for a commercial service, not demands to settle AI philosophy before a support ticket can be answered.

Anthropic's explicit exceptions are helpful. They should remain visible in enforcement and support, rather than disappearing behind a broad accusation of abuse. A user asking a paid tool to correct an error is performing ordinary quality control.

For customers, prudence means checking the exact policy that applies to their product and date, recording significant failures and keeping responsibility with a human decision-maker. It does not mean assuming a courteous phrase will authorize a prohibited task, or that terse instructions are inherently unlawful or disallowed.

---

## **Courtesy Is Sensible. Confusion Is Expensive.**

There is little practical value in spending an afternoon insulting a chatbot. Users can prefer civil interaction without claiming the software has acquired personhood. They can also demand accurate work without accepting the premise that criticism is cruelty.

The coming policy change is worth following because it puts a model-directed conduct rule into a widely used commercial service. Its stated scope is narrow, its principal enforcement mechanism is conversation ending, and its philosophical rationale remains uncertain.

The reel turns those qualifications into a more dramatic warning. The documents give customers something more useful: distinctions they can apply when evaluating their own use.

November 12 is the effective date for the forthcoming revision. It is not a deadline to replace every structured agent message with a reassuring note. Review the actual rules, preserve accountability and insist that software providers explain the consequences of their decisions. That is a better foundation for dependable AI than trying to guess whether the chatbot took offense.
