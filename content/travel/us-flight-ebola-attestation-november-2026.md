As of October 23, a new U.S. travel declaration reached far beyond flights originating in Central Africa, making the details worth reading before check-in. Under an order the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention issued October 22, the order said passengers boarding international flights to the United States would have to attest that they have not been in the Democratic Republic of the Congo during the preceding 21 days, unless a U.S. government exemption applies.

The original order specified the effective time: **11:59 p.m. Eastern Standard Time on November 3, 2026**. The trigger is the flight's departure for the United States, not simply its scheduled arrival date. The obligation was not limited to trips originating in Africa: the last departure airport did not establish a passenger’s complete travel history.

This is a border-health measure responding to a serious outbreak. It is also a new paperwork obligation imposed across international aviation. Understanding both realities is more useful than treating the order as either an assurance of perfect safety or evidence that an American outbreak has already occurred.

## The New Requirement Is Broader Than the Affected Region

The declaration asks about presence in the DRC; its collection is not confined to passengers whose last departure airport is there. The CDC's instructions say it applies to passengers intending to board international flights destined for the United States, unless exempted. The signed order also covers passengers connecting through the United States on their way to another country.

U.S. citizenship does not, by itself, remove the obligation. Reuters reported that earlier DRC-related restrictions were extended to American citizens in July. The new declaration establishes a broader collection mechanism rather than merely checking the nationality or final destination printed on a ticket.

The distinction matters because itineraries are not complete exposure histories. Someone can spend time in the DRC, travel onward, and board a U.S.-bound aircraft elsewhere. The CDC's rationale is that travel history remains relevant after the original departure airport has disappeared from the final leg of the booking.

The order's definition of aircraft includes commercial, general-aviation and private aircraft. An aircraft passing over the United States without landing is outside this order's coverage. This is not a rule solely for the familiar airline check-in kiosk.

## Three Dates Travelers Should Not Confuse

October 22 is the order's issuance date. November 3 at 11:59 p.m. EST is the effective departure-time cutoff. The third date is personal: when a traveler last left the DRC.

For the declaration's waiting period, the signed order defines the departure date from the DRC as day zero. Day one is the first full calendar day outside the country. A person who was present there may board a flight to the United States on day 22, subject to other applicable requirements.

That is more specific than casually counting three weeks backward from an arrival reservation. Travelers whose plans are close to the boundary should check the current CDC instructions and their carrier's implementation rather than relying on a headline's shorthand.

The order also operates alongside other CDC Ebola measures. It expressly says it does not supersede, modify or replace those orders. Completing this declaration is therefore not a general clearance overriding separate entry restrictions, visa requirements or other conditions of travel.

## An Airport Connection Can Count

The CDC defines presence in the DRC to include physical presence by air, land or sea. An airport connection counts even if a passenger did not disembark or leave the airport. Simply flying over the country without landing does not.

That detail is easy to miss and difficult to repair at the boarding gate. The relevant question is not whether a traveler considers the DRC a destination. It is whether the itinerary involved physical presence under the order's definition.

For passengers connecting through an American airport, the reverse point also matters: saying that the United States is only a transfer point does not eliminate the declaration requirement. Airlines have to apply the actual rule, not the passenger's informal description of the trip.

## What Passengers and Airlines Must Do

Passengers must give the airline or aircraft operator a completed, signed declaration in paper or digital form. Electronic signatures are permitted. The CDC encourages carriers to incorporate the attestation into paperless check-in, but an encouragement to integrate it is not evidence that every airline already has an identical process ready.

Each passenger needs a separate attestation. A parent or another authorized individual may sign for someone under 18. The order also permits an authorized individual to act for someone unable to act on their own behalf because of age or physical or mental impairment.

Airlines must not board a passenger who fails to provide the required declaration. The signed order requires carriers to retain it for at least 42 days after the flight departs for the United States and provide it to the government upon request. That departure-based retention language is worth noting; a secondary travel report describes the period differently.

A signature is not a formality without consequences. The CDC warns that knowingly and willfully false statements can lead to criminal penalties under federal law. Travelers should resolve uncertainty before check-in, not guess that a transit stop is too minor to mention.

## Why the Agency Is Looking Beyond Symptoms

The signed order describes Bundibugyo virus disease, a form of Ebola, as a serious illness with an incubation period typically ranging from two to 21 days. It says infected individuals do not spread the disease until symptoms begin, while also explaining that someone can travel before becoming visibly ill.

Those statements are compatible. A person may not be infectious while asymptomatic but can cross borders before the illness becomes apparent, creating a later need for diagnosis, isolation and contact tracing. Symptom checks alone cannot reconstruct that person's recent travel history.

Reuters reported October 22 that the CDC said no cases associated with this outbreak had been reported in the United States to date. That is the reported position at that time, not a promise about subsequent developments. The order itself is a preventive intervention, not proof of domestic transmission.

Readers should also avoid treating all Ebola vaccines and treatments as interchangeable. The order says no widely approved vaccines or specific antiviral treatments exist for Bundibugyo virus disease and discusses ongoing research. Results or approvals for another Ebola virus do not automatically answer the clinical question here.

## A Declaration Is a Layer, Not a Diagnosis

An attestation records a claim about travel history. It does not test a passenger for infection, independently verify every movement, or establish that every person boarding an aircraft presents no health risk.

The policy argument is that requiring everyone to answer creates a consistent checkpoint, including for complex itineraries that ordinary route-based screening might miss. The corresponding accountability question is whether the government and carriers implement that checkpoint clearly enough for accurate answers and useful enforcement.

Public safety is a legitimate national responsibility. So is explaining restrictions accurately. Broad collection should come with usable instructions, clear handling of exemptions and an honest account of what the intervention can and cannot accomplish. Calling paperwork a complete protective barrier would be as careless as dismissing travel history as irrelevant.

## What the Original Deadline Meant

For an itinerary near the original deadline, the relevant questions were the departure-time cutoff, who could sign for a child or dependent traveler, and any DRC presence within the specified period, including airport connections. A person needing an exemption could not infer one simply from citizenship or the purpose of a journey.

The November 3 deadline has passed by this recovery publication. The discussion above explains the original October 22 measure; it is not a standalone boarding checklist. A current trip requires the latest CDC instructions and the carrier’s process, including any applicable exemption or subsequent change.

## November 5 Recovery Update: A Broader Clarification

CDC’s page, updated November 3 and retrieved November 5, says an **October 28 amended order** clarified coverage of all people on covered aircraft, including operating crew and crew being repositioned. The PDF at the original signed-order URL is now titled “Notice and Amended Order” and bears an October 28 signature date. It should not be described as an unchanged copy of the October 22 order. The original order is also reproduced in the October 27 Federal Register notice.

The amended PDF retains the November 3, 11:59 p.m. EST departure cutoff and says the order lasts until rescinded or modified, or December 18, whichever comes first. Its retention provision still runs for at least 42 days after departure. CDC’s current webpage inconsistently refers to arrival in one instruction and departure in other answers. That discrepancy remains unresolved here; carriers need current agency direction rather than an assumption that those descriptions are identical.

An attestation remains a travel-history declaration, not a diagnosis or a guarantee that exposure has been eliminated. Clear implementation and truthful answers matter more than treating a signature as complete protection.
