Washington's new counter-drone agreement has two jobs, not one: confront dangerous unmanned aircraft and make sure the response does not create a different danger for civilian aviation. The second task deserves as much attention as the first.

The FAA's November 9 announcement describes an expanded interagency effort involving the departments of Transportation, War, Homeland Security, Energy and Justice. The memorandum establishes a process for evaluating counter-unmanned-aircraft systems and deploying them with aviation safety in mind.

That is a consequential coordination promise. It is not proof that every defensive technology is safe in every setting, that new legal powers have been granted or that American airspace has become impenetrable.

The useful part of the announcement is its description of the machinery: technical requirements, field testing, data sharing, safety-risk assessment and airspace deconfliction. The test now is whether that process can turn an understandable demand for security into deployments that protect the public without disrupting the aircraft the public already uses.

## **Detection and Interdiction Are Different Questions**

The FAA's statement distinguishes systems that detect or track drones from systems that stop them or take control of them. Those functions belong in the same security discussion, but they are not interchangeable.

Knowing that an aircraft is present is a different capability from determining whether it is dangerous. Deciding to intervene adds another set of questions: what action is justified, who has authority to take it and what happens to other users of the airspace?

The announcement does not supply a technical specification for every system. It would therefore be wrong to claim that every device under consideration uses the same mechanism or presents the same risks. The point of a safety assessment is to examine the actual proposed technology and its operating conditions.

The departments say their expanded collaboration is intended to ensure that counter-drone technologies do not adversely affect aircraft operations or navigation equipment. That is not a peripheral qualification. It describes a central condition of the security mission.

A defensive tool should be judged by both the threat it can address and the environment in which it can safely operate. A strong capability in the wrong place is not automatically a sound deployment.

## **The Memorandum Is a Process, Not a Performance Certificate**

According to the FAA, the memorandum was led by the White House Task Force to Restore American Airspace Sovereignty and further establishes an interagency evaluation and deployment process.

The signed memorandum's complete text is not the basis of this account; the published FAA announcement is. That distinction limits what can responsibly be said about clauses, exceptions or procedures not reproduced in the statement.

The announcement is nevertheless specific about several intended responsibilities. The participating departments will work with the FAA and Federal Communications Commission when developing new systems. They will consider transportation, aviation and communications requirements alongside testing data.

They also intend to collaborate on technical requirements, field testing and information sharing. The FAA may provide standards and guidance for safe introduction, while Transportation may address how the capabilities fit within the wider civilian transportation system.

Those are the ingredients of a coordination framework. They do not independently demonstrate its effectiveness. An agreement can improve the way agencies work together, but the document announcing it is not itself an operational test result.

## **Why the FAA and FCC Both Matter**

The explicit involvement of aviation and communications regulators is an acknowledgment that a drone defense does not operate in a vacuum. The same public sky contains legitimate flights, navigation needs and other systems whose safe operation matters.

The announcement commits the parties to consider the applicable requirements and testing data rather than treating a security label as an exemption from technical scrutiny. That is a sensible starting point. The more difficult work is applying those requirements when agencies have different missions and incentives.

A border-security team, an energy-infrastructure operator and an aviation-safety official can all have legitimate concerns without ranking each operational trade-off identically. Coordination is valuable precisely because the interests overlap without being identical.

Nothing in the published statement establishes a new blanket statutory authority for every participant. Nor does it identify a new spending total. Readers should not infer either from the presence of several cabinet departments in a joint announcement.

The narrower documented claim is that the departments are expanding a shared evaluation process. Legal powers, budget requests and particular deployment decisions would each require their own evidence.

## **Risk Assessment Has to Precede Reassurance**

The FAA says it will quickly review proposed systems and conduct safety-risk assessments to ensure protocols and airspace deconfliction measures are adequate. It will provide technical data identifying unsafe conditions and recommend ways to reduce the risks.

If the agency believes additional mitigation is necessary, the statement says it can impose protective measures, including restrictions on airspace. That provision matters because it preserves an explicit safety role alongside the operational enthusiasm in the announcement.

A restriction may be a necessary way to manage risk; it is not evidence that a system is harmless. The public deserves to understand both the benefit of a proposed defense and the operational conditions needed to use it safely.

Speed is also not the same thing as adequacy. A fast review can be useful, but the relevant question is whether it examines the right evidence. The statement's promise of quick assessment should not become a substitute for a documented assessment.

These are standards for evaluating implementation, not assertions that officials have failed to perform a particular undisclosed test. The announcement alone cannot establish what every future review will contain.

## **Official Success Claims Need Their Labels**

The release includes officials' accounts of prior counter-drone work. It cites a safety assessment of a high-energy laser system and describes earlier interagency cooperation. Officials also make claims about border deployments and drone seizures during the World Cup.

Those are attributed government statements. Their inclusion in an FAA announcement does not turn them into independently audited performance data. A reader should not mistake a quoted official's confidence for a comprehensive demonstration of effectiveness across every intended use.

The same caution applies to sweeping language about an impenetrable sky. Security can improve without reaching an absolute condition. A public agency's statement about coordination does not establish that every possible drone threat has been defeated.

This is not a reason to dismiss the threat or the work. Protecting airspace, critical infrastructure and the border is a legitimate government responsibility. Serious treatment of that responsibility calls for precise claims, not a choice between complacency and extravagant assurances.

A useful report would separate what was detected, what action was taken and what outcome was confirmed. Those are different measures, even when they appear in the same successful operation.

## **A Practical Scorecard for the Next Stage**

Readers can look for evidence that the framework is producing usable results: identified testing standards, clear agency responsibilities, documented mitigation requirements and an explanation of how civilian aviation is protected during deployment.

Those are proposed accountability measures, not reporting requirements that this article claims the memorandum already imposes. The published announcement does not promise a public performance dashboard or describe a universal disclosure schedule.

It does provide enough detail to ask better questions. Was the particular technology assessed for its intended setting? Were unsafe conditions identified? What restrictions or other mitigations were required? Who can pause an operation when circumstances change?

A capable government should be able to confront hostile drones and protect ordinary air travel at the same time. Treating those duties as enemies would lower the standard for both. The agreement's strongest premise is that security and safety have to be engineered together. Its success will be measured not by how many agencies endorsed the announcement, but by whether their shared process makes that premise work in the sky.
