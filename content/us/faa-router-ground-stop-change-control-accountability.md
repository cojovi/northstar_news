The FAA has supplied a more detailed account of the communications trouble behind the November 13 nationwide ground stop. It has not supplied a final root-cause report. The distinction should shape the accountability debate now taking place around the network.

In a November 15 question-and-answer document, the agency says its legacy equipment became unstable as data flooded the telecommunications network. It identifies two potential locations in the initial investigation and describes work by network provider L3Harris at one of them, including the deployment of a new router. Blocking the two locations stabilized the network, according to the FAA.

The agency also says it disabled the same functionality on approximately 60 similar routers as a precaution. FedScoop subsequently reported that a router was being examined in Florida with FAA personnel and FBI agents involved.

That is a stronger reporting foundation than the initial notice of communications issues. It is still not proof of a cyberattack, a finalized finding of negligence or a verdict that every modernization project is defective. The questions have become more specific. The answers should follow the evidence.

## **What the FAA Says Failed**

The agency says data reached the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure network at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. The resulting instability affected aircraft communications and radar feeds.

Its initial investigation identified a radar site in Amarillo, Texas, and a radio-communications site in El Dorado, Arkansas, as potential origins. L3Harris had been working at El Dorado, including deploying a new router. The FAA says L3Harris blocked the two locations and the network stabilized.

The wording matters. These are the agency's reported observations and preliminary interpretation, not an independently reproduced technical test by Northstar. The same FAQ says the FAA and L3Harris continue investigating the root cause.

A reader can recognize a plausible causal sequence without confusing it with a completed investigation. Which component behaved unexpectedly, why it did so, what configuration or installation conditions mattered and how the resulting traffic propagated are related questions. A short public account does not necessarily answer all of them.

## **Seventeen Centers Is a National Reliability Question**

The FAA says instability began at 17 of its 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers and spread into several Terminal Radar Approach Controls.

That makes containment central to the story. The issue is not only whether work at a remote location can go wrong. It is how a problem associated with a small number of locations can affect so much of the network used to support national air traffic.

The agency says the system was not completely without radio and radar service. Certain frequencies and feeds remained operational, allowing controllers to communicate with and track aircraft. Its account says all aircraft landed safely.

That is important safety information. It should not be flattened into a claim of total communications collapse. But safe landing capability does not answer every question about resilience. An emergency procedure can succeed while the underlying operational disruption remains unacceptable.

The [initial ground-stop account](https://thenorthstarledger.com/us/faa-ground-stop-accountability-test) concerned the immediate safety response. The newer disclosure raises a narrower engineering question: what stopped a local disturbance from becoming national, and what did not?

## **A Precaution Is Not a Universal Diagnosis**

Disabling functionality on approximately 60 similar routers is a material response. It indicates that the agency considered a common feature sufficiently concerning to restrict it beyond the initially identified locations.

It does not establish that all those routers failed, that each had the same configuration or that their hardware was irreparably defective. A precautionary action is not a finding that every component would have reproduced the incident.

The public should ask what testing will determine whether the functionality can safely return, whether a configuration change is needed and what monitoring would identify a recurrence. These are proposed questions for the investigation, not claims that the FAA has already committed to a particular answer.

Restoring the network and explaining why the failure occurred are also different milestones. The FAQ says the system became stable and air traffic resumed the same day. Stability after isolation is evidence about the immediate response, not a substitute for a complete account of the initiating conditions.

A credible repair needs both: demonstrated recovery and a defensible explanation of how recurrence risk has changed.

## **FBI Participation Does Not Prove an Attack**

FedScoop reports that Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy described FAA staff and FBI agents participating in an analysis at an L3Harris laboratory in Florida. The report attributes his account rather than presenting a completed forensic finding.

The FAA's own FAQ says it had no indication, based on available evidence, of a cyberattack or an outside malicious actor. That is a provisional statement about the evidence available to the agency, not a promise that an investigation can discover nothing new.

Both facts should remain visible. Involving federal investigators can support the integrity or scope of an inquiry without establishing a criminal cause. Their presence is not a shortcut from an equipment problem to sabotage.

The FAA also distinguishes the event from its SMART data-aggregation system, saying the systems are unrelated and that SMART does not feed information into air traffic control. Any alternative causal allegation would need evidence, not merely the observation that several technology controversies occurred around the same time.

An investigation should be allowed to investigate. Turning each participant's job title into a conclusion would make the public less informed, not more.

## **The Contractor's Percentage Is Not a Reliability Score**

L3Harris's November 14 statement says that, with telecommunications partners and the FAA, it had completed more than 70 percent of the planned conversion from legacy copper wires to fiber optics. It says the company is supporting the FAA's investigation.

That percentage is the contractor's statement about project progress. It is not an independently audited measure of end-to-end reliability, and it does not establish the cause of the ground stop.

The distinction is broadly relevant to government technology projects. A count of completed conversions can help describe work performed. It cannot, by itself, demonstrate that the combined old-and-new environment behaves safely under abnormal conditions.

The FAA says routine equipment replacement should be distinguished from the broader modernization program. That distinction should be examined rather than waved away. An incident can involve work in a transitioning system without proving that every modernization measure either caused the failure or prevented it.

The question is what the specific technical and management records establish. Neither the label “legacy” nor the label “modernization” should be treated as an automatic assignment of blame.

## **Before the Next Funding Fight, Demand the Change Record**

The FAA acknowledges that maintenance at major control facilities is typically performed at night, while routine replacement at remote sites has long occurred at other times. It says it will reevaluate when replacement and maintenance work is done.

Timing is one question. Others include predeployment testing, approval of the change, monitoring during installation, the ability to isolate unexpected behavior and the conditions for rolling back. Asking those questions does not establish that the corresponding controls were absent.

It does establish what a useful public explanation should address. The goal should be an evidence-based account of how the change was evaluated and how the resulting network behavior compared with expectations.

Additional investment may be necessary. It should come with a clear connection between the identified failure, the proposed remedy and a measurable result. An urgent request for money is not itself an engineering demonstration.

The flights can move again before the accountability work is finished. What the country needs next is not a more confident slogan about old equipment or new technology. It is a record showing what failed, why the safeguards were or were not sufficient, and how the responsible institutions will prove that the fix works.
